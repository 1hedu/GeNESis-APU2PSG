Four techniques, one PSG

The NES has two pulses with four duties, a triangle, sixteen noise periods and DPCM. The Genesis PSG has three squares and a noise generator. No single trick covers that gap — so here is which one wins where, and exactly what each costs.

64.3% of noise frames need a period the three fixed rates miss

Where each technique wins

The volume DAC is the main solution: park a channel's period at 0 so it outputs DC, then rewrite its logarithmic attenuator from a free-running Z80 loop. That buys real 12.5 / 25 / 75% duty and a real triangle staircase. What it cannot buy is pitch — a 12.5% pulse needs eight samples per period to exist at all, so the loop's sample rate divided by eight is the ceiling. Above it, the voice goes back to the hardware tone generator, where the pitch stays exact and only the duty degrades to 50%.

Moving the triangle to FM is what makes the top lane possible. Four operators in parallel at MUL 1, 3, 5, 7 are a triangle, additively — exact in pitch to a third of a cent, with none of the 2 dB quantisation that flattens the staircase's peaks on a logarithmic DAC. It costs no Z80 cycles, which shortens the loop from 233 to 144 and lifts the pulse ceiling from 1920 Hz to 3107 Hz; and it costs no PSG channel, which is what makes the noise trade below disappear.

The triangle is the one place where the volume DAC is not an upgrade but the only option. NES triangles reach down to 27 Hz; the PSG's tone generator stops at 109 Hz and does not go flat below it — it simply cannot go there.

Figure 2 · the budget

Why the ceiling sits where it does

Sample rate is loop length. That single fact is why “more voices” and “more pitch” are the same problem seen from two sides: every voice added to the loop lengthens it, which lowers the rate, which lowers the ceiling for every voice at once. So the driver ships three loop variants and the ROM picks one per frame.

Noise wanted the triangle's channel

The PSG's noise generator has three fixed rates. The NES has sixteen periods in two modes. Setting the PSG's rate selector to 3 clocks the shift register from tone channel 2 instead, and channel 2's period is 10 bits — which brings 14 of the 16 within 3%. The two fastest are out of range: they want shift-register periods below 1.

The shift register advances once per tone-3 output cycle, so its rate is clock/(32 × P). That divisor is worth stating because getting it wrong puts every drum an octave out: the three fixed rates are documented both as clock/512, /1024, /2048 and as tone periods 0x10, 0x20, 0x40, and only this reading satisfies both. It also places the fixed rates on periods 9, 11 and 13 — to within 0.8%, which is presumably why the chip has them.

The price used to be channel 2, which is where the triangle lived. With the triangle on FM there is nothing to pay, so this mode is simply on — and it stays on rate 3 for every period rather than switching back and forth, because writing the noise control register resets the shift register and a song that alternates would restart its noise pattern on every switch.

What each one costs

Hardware tone

The PSG's own square

Pitch-exact anywhere above its floor, and free. It is 50% duty and only 50% duty, and its 10-bit period cannot reach below 109 Hz.

costs nothing · gives pitch, never timbre

Volume DAC

The attenuator as a waveform

Any duty, a real triangle staircase, and pitch below the PSG's own floor. Bounded above by sample rate over steps, and by 2 dB of amplitude resolution — the staircase peaks quantise coarsely.

costs 63–89 Z80 cycles a voice · gives timbre, and the bottom octave

Tone-clocked noise

Channel 2 as a shift clock

Turns 3 reachable noise periods into 14, in both long and short mode. Short mode needs its own table: the NES sequence is 93 steps to the PSG's 15, so it matches pitch rather than clock — which also lifts the two fastest periods back into range.

costs nothing, once the triangle is on FM · gives the drum kit

Triangle, and the sampled channel

Four parallel operators are a triangle outright, at any pitch, for no Z80 time and no PSG channel. Channel 6's 8-bit linear DAC takes DPCM, which does not fit on the volume DAC — that would eat the whole loop for four logarithmic bits.

costs 2 FM channels · gives the triangle, the samples, and channel 2 back

Cycle counts come from the assembler; the percentages come from the 8,697-frame capture checked into the repository. The Z80 loop is verified by executing it instruction by instruction and checking the bytes it emits — none of this has run on real hardware yet. The two things most likely to move are the per-frame bus stall and V2D's true rate, whose PCM read picks up bank-window wait states the cycle count does not model.
